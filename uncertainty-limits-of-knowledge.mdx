The cold room logger shows 7.6 °C and the limit is 8.0 °C, so the stock is safe. Is it? The probe is accurate to half a degree, so the air may be at 8.1 °C, and the shelf by the door is warmer than the probe. Every reading, every model and every forecast has a limit to what it can tell you. This lesson shows where those limits are, and how engineers design a system so that it still works when the numbers are a little wrong. #Uncertainty #EngineeringDesign #ComplexSystems

## Every Number Has Error Bars

Error bars are the short lines on a graph that show how far the true value may lie from the plotted point. Every measured number has them, drawn or not.

Measure a steel beam with a tape and you may write 2.450 m. The true length may be 2.4497 m or 2.4503 m. The tape has a smallest division, your eye is not exactly above the mark, and the steel grows and shrinks with temperature.

<Card title="Measurement Uncertainty" icon="information">
No measurement is exact. A reported value should come with its uncertainty: the range in which the true value probably lies. When an engineer writes $2.450 \pm 0.002$ m, the $\pm 0.002$ is not a weakness. It is honesty.
</Card>

### Sources of Measurement Error

| Source | Example | What it does |
|--------|---------|-------------------|
| Instrument resolution | A thermometer that shows steps of 0.1 °C | The last digit is uncertain |
| Systematic bias | A scale that is not calibrated reads 0.5 g too high | A constant offset |
| Random noise | A flow reading that flickers | A spread around the true value |
| Drift | The temperature changes during a test of 4 hours | A slow shift |
| Effect of the instrument | Opening the cold room door to read the thermometer warms the air | The reading changes the thing measured |

### Propagation of Uncertainty

Uncertainty passes through a calculation into the result. When independent measurements are multiplied or divided, the rule is: square each percentage, add the squares, and take the square root. Two readings of 1% each give $\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = 1.4\%$, not 2%, because the two errors seldom reach their limits in the same direction together.

Stress is force divided by area, $\sigma = F / A$. If the force $F$ is known to $\pm 3\%$ and the area $A$ to $\pm 2\%$, the stress $\sigma$ is uncertain by $\sqrt{3^2 + 2^2} = 3.6\%$. That can be the difference between a safe design and a marginal one.

This is the reason for safety factors. They do not exist because engineers calculate badly. They exist because engineers are honest about how well they know their numbers.

### A worked case: is the cold room inside its limit?

A cold room must keep its stock at or below 8.0 °C. The logger shows 7.6 °C.

<Steps>
1. **List what stands between the reading and the truth.** The maker gives the probe as accurate to ±0.5 °C. The logger adds ±0.2 °C. The probe hangs near the cooling unit, and the stock by the door is warmer.

2. **Combine the independent uncertainties.** Both are in °C, so square each, add, and take the square root: $\sqrt{0.5^2 + 0.2^2} = 0.54$ °C. The air at the probe is between 7.1 and 8.1 °C.

3. **Compare with the limit.** The upper end, 8.1 °C, is above 8.0 °C. The reading does not show that the room is inside its limit. It does not show that the room is outside it.

4. **Correct the known offset.** A reference thermometer on the shelf by the door reads 0.8 °C above the probe. This is a constant offset, not a random error, so add it: $7.6 + 0.8 = 8.4$ °C, give or take 0.54 °C. The warmest stock is probably above the limit.

5. **Act on it.** Set the alarm at the limit minus the offset and the uncertainty: $8.0 - 0.8 - 0.54 = 6.66$ °C, rounded down to 6.6 °C at the probe. Or move the probe to the warmest shelf. That removes the offset, and the alarm can sit at $8.0 - 0.54 = 7.46$ °C, rounded down to 7.4 °C.
</Steps>

The display showed 7.6 °C the whole time. The number did not change. What you may conclude from it did.

### A Real-World Example: The Mars Climate Orbiter

In 1999, NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter was lost because one engineering team used imperial units (pound-force seconds) and another used metric units (newton-seconds) for the impulse of the thrusters. Nothing was wrong with the physics or the instruments. The numbers of each group were correct inside the group. The error was at the interface between them.

The combined cost of the orbiter and its companion lander mission was approximately 327.6 million USD. The cause was not a measurement error. It was an assumption about units that nobody wrote down and nobody checked.

<Card title="Units Are Assumptions" icon="warning">
Every number carries assumptions: its unit, its reference point, its sign. Two engineers can read the same undocumented number in two ways. Writing the unit beside every number is not bureaucracy. It prevents errors.
</Card>

### Statistical Thinking in Engineering

Four terms are enough to start:

| Concept | Meaning | Engineering Use |
|---------|---------|----------------|
| Mean | The average value | The central estimate of a measured quantity |
| Standard deviation | The spread of the readings around the mean | Shows how repeatable the measurement is |
| Confidence interval | The range in which the true value probably lies | Shows how far to trust the estimate |
| Outlier | A reading far from the rest | A real event, or a faulty measurement |

Do not report the mean and throw away the spread. A supply with a mean of 5.0 V and a standard deviation of 0.01 V is a different thing from one with the same mean and a standard deviation of 0.5 V.

## Chaos: Deterministic but Unpredictable

<InArticleAd />


In the early 1960s, the meteorologist Edward Lorenz ran a simple weather simulation on a computer. To save time, he started a run again from the middle and typed in the values by hand, rounding one number from 0.506127 to 0.506. The weather that came out was completely different.

It was not a fault in the program. It was chaos.

<Card title="Chaos Theory" icon="warning">
A chaotic system is deterministic: the same start always gives the same result. But a very small difference at the start grows into a very large difference later. We can never measure the start exactly, so long-range prediction is impossible in practice.
</Card>

### The Butterfly Effect in Engineering

This sensitivity to the start is called the butterfly effect. Engineers meet it in two common places.

**Weather.** Tomorrow's forecast is good, next week's is poor, and next month's is of little use. The forecasters are not at fault. The atmosphere is a chaotic system with a prediction horizon of roughly 10 to 14 days.

**Turbulent flow.** The equations of fluid flow are fully known. Yet you cannot predict where a plume of smoke will go, because two almost equal flows soon become different.

### The Practical Consequence

Some systems cannot be predicted beyond a certain time, however good the instruments and the computer. So find the prediction horizon and design inside it.

Weather forecasters run the simulation hundreds of times from slightly different starts and look at the spread. If the runs agree, confidence is high. If they do not, the forecast is known to be unreliable. A solar plant can plan tomorrow from tomorrow's forecast. Its battery must be sized from the records of many years, because no forecast reaches next month.

### Lyapunov Time: The Prediction Horizon

Each chaotic system has a typical time, called the Lyapunov time, beyond which prediction becomes unreliable:

| System | Approximate Prediction Horizon |
|--------|-------------------------------|
| Turbulent fluid flow | Seconds to minutes |
| Weather | 10 to 14 days |
| Planetary orbits (inner solar system) | Millions of years |
| Double pendulum | Seconds |
| Stock market prices | Practically zero for individual trades |

If the horizon is long compared with the time your controller needs, you can control by prediction. If it is short, you must measure and correct all the time, which is feedback. The controller of a burner with a turbulent flame cannot predict the flow 10 seconds ahead. It can measure the present state and adjust at once. The uncertainty does not make control impossible. It changes the kind of control that works.

## Complexity and Emergence

<InArticleAd />


Chaos can appear in a simple system. Complexity is different: many parts act on each other, and together they show behaviour that no single part has. This is called emergence.

### Emergent Behavior

Take traffic. Each driver follows simple rules: hold your speed, keep your distance, brake when you must. No driver intends a jam, yet jams form. The jam belongs to the whole system and to no single car.

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Ant Colonies" icon="information">
    Each ant follows simple scent rules. The colony finds the shortest path to food, which no single ant knows.
  </Card>
  <Card title="After a Power Cut" icon="warning">
    Each refrigerator starts its compressor when the power returns, which is correct for each one. Together they draw a starting current that can trip the supply again.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Evening Water Peak" icon="information">
    Each household opens a tap when it needs water. Together they pull the pressure down at the far end of the network every evening.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Internet Routing" icon="information">
    Each router decides locally where to send a packet. Congestion and spreading failures arise from millions of such decisions.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### Why Emergence Matters for Engineers

You can test a part alone. The system that contains it may still surprise you. A pump that passes every test on the bench can cause pressure surges when it works with three others on one pipe. A beam that meets its specification can take part in a resonance when it is joined to other beams.

So test the parts, then test the system, and accept that some behaviour of the whole will appear only in service.

### Tightly Coupled Systems

The sociologist Charles Perrow studied accidents in complex systems. He argued that in tightly coupled systems, where the parts act on each other quickly and with little slack, a failure can spread faster than the operators can respond.

A power grid is tightly coupled. The Northeast blackout of 2003 started with an alarm system failure at a utility in Ohio, combined with untrimmed trees touching power lines. Within hours, 55 million people lost power across the northeastern United States and Ontario, Canada.

In such a system, design for a failure that spreads, not only for a part that fails. Circuit breakers, limits on rate, and sections that can be isolated all keep a local failure local.

## Known Unknowns and Unknown Unknowns

<InArticleAd />


In 2002, the U.S. Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld gave an answer at a press conference that was widely mocked and is useful all the same:

> "There are known knowns; there are things we know we know. We also know there are known unknowns; that is to say we know there are some things we do not know. But there are also unknown unknowns, the ones we don't know we don't know."

Engineers meet all three kinds.

| Category | Definition | Engineering Example | Response |
|----------|-----------|---------------------|----------|
| Known knowns | Facts we are confident of | The yield strength of steel at room temperature | Use directly in design |
| Known unknowns | Gaps we know we have | Next year's rainfall, or the wind load in a 100-year storm | Statistics and safety factors |
| Unknown unknowns | Surprises that nobody has imagined | A way of failing that nobody thought of | Redundancy, monitoring and review |

### The Most Dangerous Category

A known unknown can be managed. You do not know the soil under a new tank stand, so you test it and add margin. You do not know how much water the village will use, so you size the tank with room to spare.

Unknown unknowns are the dangerous kind. The Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed in 1940 due to aeroelastic flutter, a wind-driven oscillation that bridge engineers had not considered. The Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster in 2011 resulted partly from a tsunami exceeding the design basis that planners had considered sufficient.

You cannot prepare for every one of them. You can build systems that fail gradually and not all at once, and you can watch for the first signs.

### Converting Unknown Unknowns to Known Unknowns

The best strategy is to turn unknown unknowns into known unknowns early. Four practices help:

- **Mixed teams.** A mechanical engineer, an electrician and an operator see different risks in the same system.
- **A pre-mortem.** Before the project starts, ask the team: "Imagine that this project has failed badly. What went wrong?" People explain a failure more easily than they predict one.
- **Other people's failures.** Many failures have a twin in another industry. The NASA Lessons Learned database and the investigation reports of the U.S. Chemical Safety Board are free to read.
- **Monitoring in service.** A system in the field shows how it really behaves. An unexpected pattern or a value out of range can reveal an unknown before it becomes a failure.

## The Limits of Simulation

<InArticleAd />


A simulation is a model run on a computer, and every model leaves something out. The lesson on [models, maps and reality](/education/philosophy-of-science-engineering/models-maps-reality/) deals with that in full. Three points belong here.

### What Simulations Leave Out

A bridge under a steady load can be simulated with high accuracy. A real bridge also meets:

<Steps>
1. **Wind gusts** that change direction and strength
2. **Daily heating and cooling** over decades
3. **Corrosion** that slowly removes metal
4. **Fatigue** from millions of load cycles
5. **Settling foundations** as the soil changes with moisture
6. **Overloads**, such as a driver who ignores the weight limit
7. **Missed maintenance** when inspections are cut to save money
</Steps>

A good simulation may include items 1 to 4. Items 6 and 7 are human decisions, and no physical simulation contains them.

### Verification and Validation

Two different questions are asked of a simulation.

**Verification:** Does the program solve the equations correctly? This is a question of mathematics. Compare the program with a case that has a known exact answer.

**Validation:** Do the equations describe the real world? This is a question of physics. Only a comparison with measurements can answer it.

A simulation can be verified and still be invalid: it solves the wrong equations perfectly.

### The Danger of Black Box Tools

Modern simulation tools are easy to use. A new engineer can run a stress analysis in an afternoon. That is a gift and a danger, because the tool gives a believable picture even when the model is wrong. A wrong support, a wrong material or a mesh that is too coarse all give numbers that look reasonable.

The defense is a second, independent estimate. If the simulation shows a stress of 50 MPa and a handbook formula gives 200 MPa, one of them is wrong. Find out which before you trust either.

## Heisenberg for Engineers

<InArticleAd />


The Heisenberg uncertainty principle of quantum mechanics says that the position and the momentum of a particle cannot both be known exactly at the same time. It is a limit of nature, not of technology.

Engineers meet a different problem that looks similar, called the observer effect: measuring a system often changes it.

### Probe Effects in Electronics

An oscilloscope probe adds a small capacitance (typically 10 to 15 pF) and a parallel resistance (typically 10 megaohms) to the point it touches. On a slow circuit this changes nothing. On a fast, sensitive circuit the probe changes the signal, and you see a waveform that does not exist when the probe is removed. The measurement is accurate. It is a measurement of a different circuit.

### Observer Effects on Site

The same happens outside the laboratory. You open the cold room door to read the thermometer, and warm air enters. A flow meter adds a little resistance to the pipe it measures. A program that records how long each task takes makes each task slower.

### The Hawthorne Effect and Beyond

People change as well. Workers at the Hawthorne Works factory in the 1920s and 1930s improved their productivity because they knew they were being observed. The changes to lighting and breaks mattered less than the attention. This is called the Hawthorne effect. An operator who knows that the fuel log will be checked this week fills it in more carefully this week.

The place of a test also differs from the place of use. A device tested in a clean laboratory behaves differently in a dusty factory.

### Real Engineering Consequences of Observer Effects

| Domain | Observer Effect | Impact |
|--------|----------------|--------|
| Electronics | The probe loads the circuit | Fast signals are distorted |
| Cold storage | The door is opened to read the thermometer | The reading is higher than the undisturbed air |
| Software | The measuring tool slows the program | The slow part appears to be somewhere else |
| Mechanical testing | A strain gauge stiffens the surface locally | The measured strain is slightly low |
| People | Operators behave differently when watched | The test does not show normal practice |

### The Engineering Response

Do not stop measuring. Know how your measurement disturbs the system, and reduce the disturbance: a probe with low capacitance, a logger that reads the cold room without opening the door, a test site that matches the real one.

Where the effect cannot be removed, write it down. "Read 30 seconds after the door was opened" is far more useful than a bare number.

## Designing for Uncertainty

<InArticleAd />


If knowledge is always incomplete, how do you build a system that works all the same? Engineers use four tools.

### Safety Margins

The simplest tool is margin. If the calculated stress is 200 MPa and the material yields at 300 MPa, the safety factor is $300 / 200 = 1.5$. The margin absorbs the uncertainty in the calculation, the uncertainty in the material, and some of the unknown unknowns.

Fields with different uncertainties use different safety factors:

| Application | Typical Safety Factor | Reason |
|-------------|----------------------|--------|
| Aircraft structures | 1.5 | Weight is critical; extensive testing compensates |
| Buildings | 2.0 to 3.0 | Long service life, variable loads, less testing |
| Pressure vessels | 3.0 to 4.0 | Catastrophic failure mode, hard to inspect internally |
| Elevator cables | 8.0 to 12.0 | Human life depends on it, degradation over time |

### Redundancy

A margin protects against a load that is larger than expected. Redundancy protects against a part that fails: a second pump, a second engine, a second disk.

Redundancy works only if the two parts cannot fail from one cause. Two pumps fed through one control panel both stop when the panel fails. Two programs that use the same method share the same error.

### Fail-Safe Design

<Card title="Fail-Safe vs. Fail-Secure vs. Fail-Operational" icon="information">
**Fail-safe:** On failure, the system moves to a safe state (a traffic light shows red in all directions). **Fail-secure:** On failure, the system stays locked (a door locks when the power is lost). **Fail-operational:** On failure, the system continues to work, possibly with less capability (an aircraft with redundant flight computers).
</Card>

The right choice depends on the use. A valve on a chemical line should close on failure. A fire exit should open. The flight controls of an aircraft must continue to work, because at 35,000 feet there is no safe state to stop in.

### Defense in Depth

No single tool covers every uncertainty. Strong designs use several layers:

<Steps>
1. **Safety margin** absorbs the expected uncertainty in loads and materials
2. **Redundancy** covers the failure of a part
3. **Monitoring** shows when real conditions approach the limits of the design
4. **Fail-safe mechanisms** act when monitoring finds a critical condition
5. **Maintenance** stops slow wear from weakening the other four
</Steps>

This is called defense in depth. It is used in nuclear engineering, in aviation and wherever failure is severe. No layer is perfect, but all layers failing together is far less likely than any one layer failing alone.

## "How Confident Should I Be?" as a Design Question

<InArticleAd />


Every design decision rests on trust in something. When you choose a bolt, you trust the maker's specification. When you size a pump, you trust the water level that somebody gave you. Make that trust explicit.

### A Practical Framework

<Steps>
1. **List your assumptions.** Which facts do you rely on, and where did they come from?
2. **Rate each one.** Is it a textbook value, a maker's datasheet, one test, or a guess?
3. **Find the critical ones.** Which assumptions would make the design fail if they were wrong?
4. **Deal with the critical ones you trust least.** Test them, add margin, or change the design so that the assumption no longer matters.
5. **Write it all down.** The next engineer, who may be you, must know what was assumed and why.
</Steps>

### The Confidence Spectrum

Not all knowledge deserves the same trust:

| Level | Description | Example |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| Textbook physics | Tested for centuries | Ohm's law, beam bending equations |
| Maker's datasheet | Tested by the maker, under the maker's conditions | The tensile strength of an alloy, a pump curve |
| Simulation result | Only as good as the model and its inputs | Stress analysis of a custom bracket |
| Expert judgment | Informed opinion without firm data | "This should carry the load" |
| Extrapolation | Known data extended beyond its range | Performance at a temperature never tested |

The lower the row, the more margin you should add.

## Exercises

<InArticleAd />


1. Take a measurement you made recently (a voltage, a temperature, a weight). Estimate its uncertainty from three or more sources: the resolution of the instrument, the conditions, and your method. Combine them.

2. Read about the collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge. What was the unknown unknown? How would wind tunnel tests have found it? What might bridge engineers still be missing today?

3. Take a simulation or a calculation you have used. List three things it assumes that reality does not guarantee. For each, estimate how far the result would move if the assumption were wrong.

4. Choose something you use daily (a phone, a car, a lift, a building). Find two safety margins, two redundancies and one fail-safe mechanism in it. Which uncertainty does each one absorb?

## Summary

<InArticleAd />


- No reading is exact. Report the uncertainty, correct the known offsets, and set limits with a margin for the rest.
- A chaotic system can be predicted only as far as its horizon. Beyond that, measure and correct.
- A whole system can behave in ways that none of its parts shows. Test the system as well as the parts.
- Some risks cannot be listed in advance. Margins, redundancy, fail-safe design and monitoring are the answer to them.
- Ask "how confident should I be?" of every number that a decision depends on.
