A borehole pump trips at night for the third week. One technician wants a full study before anyone touches it. Another has already raised the overload setting "to see what happens". The first leaves the village with an unreliable pump for a month, and the second may burn the motor. Good engineering work lies between them, and it changes with what is at risk. This last lesson gathers the course into a few habits that you can use on any fault or decision, and shows how to choose how much care each one deserves. #ScientificThinking #EngineeringPractice #IntellectualHonesty

## Two Questions That Define Two Disciplines

Science and engineering ask different questions.

The scientist asks: **"Is this true?"** The goal is to understand the world as it is, whether or not the understanding is useful today.

The engineer asks: **"Is this good enough?"** The goal is to build something that works within limits of time, cost, materials and human needs. An engineer who designs a bridge is not looking for new physics. The aim is to get people safely across a river.

<Card title="The Synthesis" icon="information">
Good engineering uses both questions. Rigor keeps your work based on reality and not on hope. Pragmatism gets the work built and used.
</Card>

### Where the Two Questions Diverge

| Situation | Asking "is this true?" | Asking "is this good enough?" |
|-----------|---------------------|---------------------|
| Measuring a voltage | Repeat 100 times, calculate the standard deviation, report a confidence interval | Measure once with a calibrated meter and check that it is within the limit |
| A pump that delivers less than its curve | Study it until the cause is understood | Check whether the tank still fills. If it does, record the fact and continue |
| Testing a prototype | Vary one thing at a time across the whole range | Test whether it works under the expected conditions |
| Finding an anomaly | Investigate. Anomalies are where discoveries hide | Assess the effect on function and safety. If there is none, record it and continue |

```text
  RIGOR                                   SPEED
  "Is this true?"         "Is this good enough?"
  ├──────────┬──────────┬──────────┬──────────┤
  safety of  hard to    daily      trial on   quick
  people     undo       operation  one unit   check
```

The more a failure would cost, and the harder the decision is to undo, the further to the left the work belongs.

Neither question is wrong. The mistake is to ask the wrong one for the situation: weeks of study on a setting that can be changed tomorrow, or a quick guess on a part that keeps people safe.

## When to Be Rigorous

<InArticleAd />


Some work needs full rigor, whatever the schedule says.

### Safety-Critical Systems

If a failure of your design can injure or kill someone, rigor is not optional. The Therac-25 and the 737 MAX, examined in the [lesson on ethics](/education/philosophy-of-science-engineering/ethics-responsibility-engineering/), show what follows when it is cut. For such work:

- every assumption is written down and justified
- every calculation is checked by a second person
- every test is planned to find failure
- every anomaly is investigated, not dismissed

### Results that others will rely on

A commissioning report, a datasheet value or a published result is used by people who cannot check it. It must meet the scientific standard:

<Steps>
1. **Reproducibility.** Another person can repeat the test and get the same result.
2. **Enough data.** A single measurement is an anecdote.
3. **Honest reporting.** Include the results that did not support your expectation, and name the tests that were not done.
4. **Stated uncertainty.** Reporting $3.7 \pm 0.2$ GPa is useful. Reporting "about 3.7 GPa" is not.
</Steps>

### Decisions that are hard to undo

The depth at which a pump is set, the size of a buried pipe, and the structure of a system that will run for years are examples. An error here is paid for over years. Spend enough time to find the requirements that cannot change later, to compare the options with data, and to record the decision with its reasons.

## When to Be Pragmatic

<InArticleAd />


Other work needs speed. The skill is to know which kind is in front of you.

### Prototypes

A prototype exists to answer one question: does this idea work? It needs to answer quickly. It does not need to be tidy.

<Card title="Prototype Mindset" icon="information">
The best prototype teaches the most for the least effort. If a breadboard and a few wires can answer the question, do not design a circuit board. If one sensor taped to one machine can answer it, do not equip the whole plant.
</Card>

### Internal Tools

A script or a spreadsheet used only by your team does not need the finish of a product. It needs to be correct for the inputs it really receives, and to fail loudly, not silently, when it meets anything else. Match the care to the cost of failure. A tool that you can simply run again is different from a tool whose error corrupts the site's records.

### Quick trials

When you compare several options, such as sensor positions or alarm limits, cover them all quickly first. Then test the most promising ones with full care.

## The Art of "Good Enough"

<InArticleAd />


Every improvement has diminishing returns: the first effort brings most of the gain. Knowing when to stop is a skill.

### The Optimization Curve

A firmware engineer shortens the time that one function takes to read a sensor:

| Stage | Time spent | Execution time | Improvement |
|-------------------|-----------|---------------|-------------|
| Unoptimized baseline | 0 hours | 850 microseconds | Baseline |
| Obvious fixes | 2 hours | 320 microseconds | 62% improvement |
| A better algorithm | 8 hours | 180 microseconds | 44% further improvement |
| Low-level tuning | 20 hours | 140 microseconds | 22% further improvement |
| Tricks for one processor | 40 hours | 120 microseconds | 14% further improvement |

If the requirement is "under 500 microseconds", the engineer should stop after the obvious fixes. If it is "under 200 microseconds", after the better algorithm. The first 2 hours removed 530 microseconds. The last 40 hours removed 20.

### When "Good Enough" Is Not Good Enough

Some fields need the last few percent. Every gram matters in a spacecraft, and every milliamp-hour matters in a medical device that runs on a battery. The test is the same everywhere: when a further improvement costs more than it is worth, stop.

### The Danger of Premature Optimization

Donald Knuth's observation applies far beyond software: "Premature optimization is the root of all evil." Improve only after you have measured where the loss is. An engineer who lightens a bracket before testing the whole assembly may find that the bracket never carried the load they assumed.

### Technical Debt as a Design Decision

A shortcut taken for speed is a debt. Like a loan, it can be a good decision. The danger is the debt that nobody recorded.

<Card title="Managing Technical Debt" icon="information">
Write down every shortcut. "The alarm delay is fixed at 30 minutes because this room never defrosts for longer" tells the next engineer exactly what to check when the room changes. An unrecorded shortcut is a trap.
</Card>

## Building a Personal Scientific Practice

<InArticleAd />


Five habits carry the course into daily work. None needs a laboratory.

### 1. Keep an Engineering Notebook

Scientists keep laboratory notebooks. Keep one for your work, with four kinds of entry:

- A hypothesis, written before you investigate: "I think the readings drift because of temperature."
- What you did, exactly: "Logged the sensor beside a reference thermometer every hour from 06:00 to 18:00."
- The data, and not only your reading of it. You may read it differently later.
- Each decision and its reason: "I chose X over Y because of Z."

<Card title="Notebook Formats" icon="information">
A paper notebook, a text file for each project and a shared page all work. What matters is that you write as things happen, not from memory some days later.
</Card>

### 2. Write Your Assumptions Down

Every design and every rule rests on assumptions. In someone's head they cannot be examined. On paper they become statements that can be tested.

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Implicit Assumption">
"The sensor is accurate enough."

This cannot be tested, because "accurate enough" is not defined, and nobody who reviews the design can see the assumption.
</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Explicit Assumption">
"The BME280 temperature sensor has an accuracy of plus or minus 1 degree C over the 0 to 65 C range, which is sufficient for our application because the control algorithm only needs 2 degree C resolution."

This can be checked against the datasheet and tested. If the requirement changes to 0.5 degree C, the assumption is seen at once to fail.
</TabItem>
</Tabs>

The bearing rule in the [lesson on paradigm shifts](/education/philosophy-of-science-engineering/paradigm-shifts-how-knowledge-evolves/) failed through an assumption that nobody had written down.

### 3. Seek Disconfirmation

Confirmation bias is the tendency to look for evidence that supports what you already believe. The remedy, from the [lesson on falsifiability](/education/philosophy-of-science-engineering/falsifiability-testing-failure/), is to try to prove yourself wrong before the work leaves your hands.

<Steps>
1. **Before the test, write what failure looks like.** "More than 6 starts an hour", or "air above 5 °C for more than 30 minutes".
2. **Design tests that can fail.** A test that always passes is a ritual. Test at the limits and in the worst conditions.
3. **When a test passes, ask why.** Is the design good, or is the test weak?
4. **When you find a fault, ask what else is wrong.** Faults are seldom alone.
</Steps>

### 4. Share Your Failures

Most organizations celebrate successes and hide failures. Failures teach more, because they show where your knowledge ends. A review after a failure should look for what happened and what to change, not for someone to blame. You can:

- record the approaches that failed in your notebook, beside the one that worked
- mention dead ends in reviews: "We tried X first, and it failed because Y"
- write up a significant failure for the people who come after you

### 5. Practice Calibrated Confidence

Calibrated confidence means that when you say you are 90% sure of something, you are right about 90% of the time. Most people are overconfident. You can improve with practice:

<Steps>
1. Before you investigate a fault, write your hypothesis and your confidence: "70% sure that the cause is the supply voltage".
2. Afterwards, record whether you were right.
3. After some months, compare what you claimed with how often you were right.
4. Adjust your next estimates.
</Steps>

A person with calibrated confidence knows when to act on their own judgment and when to get more data.

## A worked case: the pump that trips at night

A borehole pump has tripped on overload 9 times in 30 days. The operator resets it each morning. Someone proposes to raise the overload setting so that the trips stop.

<Steps>
1. **Ask who is affected, and "and then what?".** The overload relay protects the motor. With a higher setting the trips stop, and the motor runs hot until it burns. The village then has no water for days. A higher setting hides the question and answers nothing.

2. **State a claim that can be wrong.** "The supply voltage falls in the evening, the motor draws more current, and the relay trips." If this is true, the trips come in the evening and at no other time.

3. **Write the assumptions.** The times in the operator's log are correct. Nothing else changed during the month.

4. **Look for the evidence against the claim.** The log shows 7 trips between 18:00 and 22:00, and 2 at midday. The claim explains 7 of 9, which is 78%. The 2 trips at midday do not fit it.

5. **State your confidence.** "About 70% that low voltage is the main cause. Something else causes the trips at midday, and I do not know what."

6. **Choose the rigor.** A new motor is expensive and hard to undo. A week of recording is cheap and harms nothing. Ask a licensed electrician to record the voltage and the current for 7 days, and write the test first: "if every trip comes with low voltage, the claim stands".

7. **Decide.** Leave the overload setting where it is. Record for one week. Decide about the supply or the pump when the record is complete.
</Steps>

Each step is a habit from this course, used on one fault. None needed special equipment, only a written claim, a search for the evidence against it, and an honest statement of what is not yet known.

## Feynman's "Cargo Cult Science"

<InArticleAd />


In his 1974 Caltech commencement address, physicist Richard Feynman described what he called "cargo cult science". During World War II, islanders in the South Pacific saw cargo planes land at military bases with supplies. After the war, some islanders built copies of runways, with bamboo control towers and wooden headphones, and hoped that the planes would return.

The planes did not come. The islanders had copied the form of the activity without its substance.

<Card title="Cargo Cult Engineering" icon="warning">
Cargo cult engineering is the ritual of good practice without its purpose. Tests that are designed to pass. A log sheet on which every box is always filled and every value is always within limits. Reviews in which nobody disagrees. Data that is collected and never read.
</Card>

### The Antidote: Intellectual Honesty

Feynman's central point was about honesty with yourself:

> "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool."

For an engineer this means:

- Report all the results. If three tests passed and one failed, report four.
- Do not hide behind a procedure. Following ISO 9001 does not make your product good. It means that you recorded what you did. The product is good if it works safely and reliably.
- Say what you do not know. "I think this is fine, but I am not sure" is more useful than false confidence.
- Do not choose among the data. If the best of ten runs meets the requirement and the average does not, the requirement is not met.
- Credit your sources.

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Science Gives You the Method">
Form a hypothesis, test it, collect data, revise. For an engineer this means: do not guess when you can measure, and do not assume when you can test.
</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Philosophy Gives You the Awareness">
Philosophy of science shows where reasoning fails: confirmation bias, a model taken for reality, a paradigm that has been outgrown, the limits of what can be known. For an engineer this means: question your assumptions, and design for what you cannot know.
</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Engineering Gives You the Purpose">
Engineering turns knowledge into things that people use, within limits of time, cost and material. The purpose is practical: something that works, is safe, and does no needless harm.

## A Final Thought

<InArticleAd />


The things engineers build shape how people live and work. The scientist-engineer asks "Can I build this?" and then continues: "Should I build it? What happens when I do? Who is affected? What do I not know? Am I being honest with myself?"

### The Compound Effect of Small Habits

None of these habits needs a large change in how you work. A notebook takes five minutes a day. An assumption takes thirty seconds to write. Seeking disconfirmation means one more test.

Kept for years, they add up: a record that you can search of the problems you have solved, and a judgment that you have checked against results.

## Exercises

<InArticleAd />


1. Choose a recent engineering decision of yours (a component, a setting, a supplier, a design). Did you use rigor, pragmatism or both? Looking back, was the level of rigor right for what was at risk?

2. Start an engineering notebook if you do not have one. For two weeks, record every hypothesis, every test and every decision on one project. Then read your notes. Which assumptions had you never written down before?

3. Read Feynman's "Cargo Cult Science" address (it is short and available online). Identify three examples of cargo cult engineering that you have seen. For each, describe the ritual and the purpose that it should serve.

4. Take the table of eight habits. For each one, write one case from your own work in the last year where it was missing. Choose one habit to practice this month.

5. Write one page on how you work. When do you choose rigor, and when speed? How do you handle a conflict between safety and schedule? Where are you most likely to fool yourself? The page is for you alone.

## Summary

<InArticleAd />


Science asks whether something is true, and engineering asks whether it is good enough. The skill is to match the rigor to the cost of being wrong and to how hard the decision is to undo. Use full rigor where people can be hurt, where others rely on your result, and where a choice cannot be reversed. Use speed for prototypes, team tools and quick trials. Five habits carry this into daily work: a notebook, written assumptions, a search for the evidence against your own claim, shared failures and calibrated confidence. Feynman's warning stands over all of them: do not fool yourself.
