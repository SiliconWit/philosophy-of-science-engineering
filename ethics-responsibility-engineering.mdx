The commissioning sheet for a new cold room has six lines, and one of them says that the high temperature alarm was tested. It was not tested. The client is waiting, and your supervisor says: "Sign it. It always works." Six months later the alarm fails silently on a hot weekend, and the sheet with your name on it says that it worked. Most ethical questions in engineering arrive in this ordinary way. This lesson sets out what an engineer owes, shows three well known disasters in which those duties were broken, and takes you through one case of your own, step by step. #EngineeringEthics #Responsibility #SafetyFirst

## The Weight of Engineering Decisions

When you choose a part, set an alarm limit or sign a test sheet, you decide something for people who will never know your name: the operator who trusts the alarm, the family that drinks the water, the patient under the machine.

<Card title="Core Principle" icon="warning">
Engineering ethics is practical. It is the discipline of making technical decisions that protect people, and it rests on five duties.
</Card>

1. Put the safety of people first.
2. Be honest about what was measured and what was not.
3. Work within your competence, and say when a task is outside it.
4. Speak up when you see a danger.
5. Keep records that are true.

The three cases that follow are real and well documented. In each one, look for the duty that was broken.

## Case Study: The Therac-25

<InArticleAd />


Between 1985 and 1987, the Therac-25 radiation therapy machine delivered massive radiation overdoses to at least six patients. Three of them died.

### What Happened

The Therac-25 treated cancer with a beam of radiation. It had a low power mode, and a high power mode in which a metal target had to stand in the path of the beam.

In the earlier models, hardware interlocks (physical devices that block a dangerous state) made it impossible to fire at high power without the target. The Therac-25 removed these interlocks and relied on software alone.

The software had a race condition, a fault in which the result depends on the timing of events. If the operator changed the mode quickly, the machine could fire at high power with the target out of the path. The patient received a dose hundreds of times higher than intended.

### What Went Wrong Technically

| Technical failure | Description |
|------------------|-------------|
| Race condition | The software did not keep the mode and the beam energy in step |
| No hardware interlocks | A safety function was left to software alone |
| Poor error messages | The machine showed "MALFUNCTION" with a number that operators could not interpret |
| No dose monitoring | No independent hardware checked the dose that was delivered |
| Code reused without review | Software from earlier models was carried over without a full analysis of the changed hardware |

### What Went Wrong Ethically

The faults were serious. The conduct around them was worse.

- When hospitals reported overdoses, the maker, AECL, at first insisted that the machine could not deliver one. It trusted its software more than the evidence from patients.
- Early reports blamed the operators. The operators knew that something was wrong and lacked the information to show it.
- The software had no independent safety review. The team that wrote it also reviewed it.
- At the time, the FDA had no clear framework for software in medical devices. The incidents led directly to changes in its regulation.

<Card title="Lesson" icon="information">
When software controls a dangerous function, treat it with the same care as a hardware safety device. A site makes the Therac-25 choice when a controller program is the only thing between a pump and dry running, with no float switch behind it. And when the field reports that your product has hurt someone, the duty is to investigate, not to defend.
</Card>

## Case Study: Boeing 737 MAX

<InArticleAd />


In October 2018 and March 2019, two Boeing 737 MAX aircraft crashed, killing 346 people. A software system called MCAS (Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System) repeatedly pushed the nose of the aircraft down, acting on a single faulty sensor reading.

### The Technical Problem

The 737 MAX had larger engines than the earlier 737 NG, mounted further forward and higher on the wing. At a high angle of attack (AoA), which is the angle between the wing and the oncoming air, the new engine position tended to push the nose up.

Boeing's answer was MCAS: software that pushed the nose down when an AoA sensor showed a high angle. It made the 737 MAX feel like the 737 NG to pilots, and so avoided expensive retraining in a full simulator.

### The Chain of Failures

<Steps>
1. **Single sensor dependency.** MCAS relied on a single AoA sensor. The 737 MAX had two AoA sensors, but on any one flight MCAS used only one of them (the two were used on alternate flights, and never compared). If that sensor failed or gave erroneous data, MCAS would activate based on false information.
2. **Excessive authority.** In its original design, MCAS could move the horizontal stabilizer by 0.6 degrees per activation. After flight testing, Boeing increased this to 2.5 degrees and allowed repeated activations, which gave the system enough authority to overpower the pilots.
3. **No pilot notification.** MCAS operated silently. The light that would have told pilots that the two AoA sensors disagreed was an optional feature, and most airlines did not buy it.
4. **Minimal training.** Boeing marketed the 737 MAX as needing only a short course on a tablet. MCAS was not mentioned in the first training materials.
5. **Weak oversight.** Under a program called Organization Designation Authorization (ODA), Boeing had been given authority to approve many aspects of the aircraft on behalf of the regulator, the FAA. Engineers within Boeing who raised safety concerns were overruled by management focused on schedule and cost.
</Steps>

### Comparison with Airbus Philosophy

Airbus A320 family aircraft use three AoA sensors. The flight control computers compare all three and use a voting scheme to reject a faulty reading. The 737 MAX had two sensors and gave only one to MCAS.

The same rule holds on any site: an action that matters should not rest on one sensor that nothing checks.

### The Ethical Dimensions

- Engineers raised concerns and were not heard. Internal messages released during the investigations showed that Boeing engineers and test pilots had serious concerns about MCAS. One test pilot described the system as "egregious" in a 2016 message.
- Money shaped the safety decisions. Boeing needed to compete quickly with the Airbus A320neo. Modifying the existing 737 was faster and cheaper than a new aircraft, and it introduced the behavior that MCAS was designed to hide.
- Transparency was given up. Boeing did not fully disclose MCAS to airlines, pilots or regulators. After the first crash (Lion Air 610), Boeing issued a bulletin and did not ground the fleet. The second crash (Ethiopian Airlines 302) happened five months later.

<Card title="Lesson" icon="warning">
When pressure of time and cost conflicts with safety, safety must win. If you raise a danger to life and are overruled, your duty is not finished: take it to the next person who has the authority to act.
</Card>

## Case Study: Volkswagen Emissions Scandal

<InArticleAd />


In September 2015, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency revealed that Volkswagen had installed software in 11 million diesel vehicles worldwide that detected an emissions test and reduced nitrogen oxide (NOx) emissions during the test. In normal driving the vehicles emitted up to 40 times the legal limit of NOx.

### How It Worked

The "defeat device" was software in the engine control unit. It watched the steering wheel position, the speed and other signals. When the pattern matched a standard test, with the wheels turning and the steering wheel fixed, it switched to a low emissions mode. On the road it favored performance and fuel economy.

### The Engineering Ethics

This was not a bug or an oversight. Engineers wrote, reviewed and tested software whose purpose was to deceive.

- "I was told to" is not an excuse. Several VW engineers said that they were following orders from management. The codes of engineering ethics are explicit: the first obligation is to the public, not to the employer.
- The harm was real. Researchers at MIT estimated that the excess emissions contributed to approximately 59 premature deaths in the United States (from the vehicles sold there) and approximately 1,200 in Europe (from the vehicles sold in Germany).
- The denial lasted. VW denied the defeat device for over a year after researchers at West Virginia University found the difference between test results and emissions on the road.

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| Vehicles affected | About 11 million worldwide |
| Duration of deception | 2009 to 2015 (at least 6 years) |
| Financial penalties | Over 30 billion USD in fines and settlements |
| Criminal convictions | Several engineers and executives sentenced to prison |

<Card title="Lesson" icon="warning">
A test that has been arranged to pass says nothing about the product. No instruction from an employer justifies a result that is made to deceive the people who rely on it.
</Card>

Few engineers will ever write a defeat device. Many will be asked to record a test that was not done, which is a small form of the same act.

## A worked case: the reading you did not take

You have commissioned a cold room for a food store. The handover sheet has six lines. You saw five of the tests. The sixth line reads "High temperature alarm tested and received on the night guard's phone", and that test was not done, because the SIM card for the alarm unit has not arrived. Your supervisor says: "Sign it. It is the same unit that we always fit, and the client pays on the signed sheet."

<Steps>
1. **Name who relies on the line.** The owner will fill the room with stock because of this sheet. If the alarm does not reach a person, a failure on a weekend is found on Monday.

2. **Separate what you measured from what you believe.** Measured: the room reached 4 °C and held it for 24 hours, and the alarm unit has power. Believed and not measured: that an alarm message reaches a phone. "It always works" is a statement about other units.

3. **List the choices.** Sign all six lines, which makes one of them false. Refuse to sign anything, which hides five true results. Or sign the five and write on the sixth: "Not tested. SIM card not fitted. The alarm does not reach a person until this test is done."

4. **Reduce the risk while the gap is open.** Propose that a named person reads the room thermometer twice a day and writes it down until the alarm is tested. Set the date of the test.

5. **Tell the people who must know.** The supervisor and the owner receive the same sheet. If the supervisor asks you to remove the note, say plainly that you cannot sign for a test you did not see.

6. **Decide.** You sign the five lines, record the sixth as not tested, and return in one week to test it.
</Steps>

The cost today is a difficult conversation and perhaps a late payment. In return the sheet says what is true, and the person who relies on it knows what it does not cover. Nobody was accused and nothing was reported outside the company. Most of an engineer's ethics is of this ordinary kind.

## Professional Codes of Ethics

<InArticleAd />


The five duties are not this lesson's invention. Engineering societies publish codes of ethics, and their main points agree.

### Common Principles

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="IEEE Code of Ethics">
The IEEE Code of Ethics requires members to:

- Hold paramount the safety, health, and welfare of the public
- Avoid real or perceived conflicts of interest
- Be honest and realistic in stating claims based on available data
- Reject bribery in all its forms
- Improve understanding of technology, its appropriate application, and potential consequences
- Maintain and improve technical competence
- Seek, accept, and offer honest criticism of technical work
- Treat all persons fairly and with respect
</TabItem>
<TabItem label="NSPE Code of Ethics">
The National Society of Professional Engineers states:

- Engineers shall hold paramount the safety, health, and welfare of the public
- Engineers shall perform services only in areas of their competence
- Engineers shall issue public statements only in an objective and truthful manner
- Engineers shall act for each employer or client as faithful agents or trustees
- Engineers shall avoid deceptive acts
- Engineers shall conduct themselves honorably, responsibly, ethically, and lawfully
</TabItem>
<TabItem label="The Common Thread">
Every major code of engineering ethics puts the same principle first: **the safety of the public comes before loyalty to an employer, personal gain or convenience.**

This is not a suggestion. Where engineering is a licensed profession, an engineer who breaks this principle can lose the license.
</TabItem>
</Tabs>

### When Codes Conflict with Reality

The codes are clear on paper. On site they meet pressure:

- Your supervisor asks you to sign for a test that you did not see.
- The client wants the pump started today, and its dry running protection is not yet wired.
- You find that an alarm fitted after an accident has been silenced, because it sounded too often.

The codes give the same answer each time: the safety of people first, and no false statement. They do not say that this is free. People who follow them sometimes pay for it, and so the way in which you raise a concern matters.

## Whistleblowing: When and How

<InArticleAd />


Whistleblowing means reporting a danger, an illegal act or an ethical violation to an authority, a regulator or the public after the channels inside the organization have failed. It is the last step of speaking up. Most concerns are settled long before it:

1. Tell the person who can fix the problem, on the same day, in plain words.
2. If nothing changes, write to your supervisor: what you saw, what you measured, what could happen.
3. If the danger remains, take the same note to the next level of authority or to the person responsible for safety.

### When Is Whistleblowing Justified?

Most ethics frameworks agree on five conditions:

<Steps>
1. The threat to the safety or welfare of people is serious and specific.
2. You know of it through your professional competence, not through rumor.
3. You have tried the internal channels, and they have failed.
4. You have recorded the concern and the organization's response.
5. The harm of staying silent is greater than the harm of reporting.
</Steps>

### The Personal Cost

Whistleblowers face real consequences. Roger Boisjoly, a Morton Thiokol engineer, warned against launching the Space Shuttle Challenger in cold weather because the O-ring seals had not been tested at low temperatures. Management overruled him. The shuttle broke apart 73 seconds after launch, killing all seven crew members.

After the disaster, Boisjoly testified before a presidential commission. He was praised publicly but effectively blacklisted within the aerospace industry. He suffered depression and PTSD. He spent the rest of his career advocating for engineering ethics education.

### Protecting Whistleblowers

Many countries have laws meant to protect people who report in good faith:

| Jurisdiction | Key legislation |
|-------------|----------------|
| United States | Whistleblower Protection Act, Sarbanes-Oxley (for publicly traded companies) |
| European Union | EU Whistleblower Directive (2019) |
| United Kingdom | Public Interest Disclosure Act |

These laws differ from country to country, and so does their enforcement. Find out what applies where you work before you need it. No law removes the personal cost.

## Design for Safety

<InArticleAd />


The best time to meet these duties is at the design stage, before anyone has to speak up.

### Fail-Safe, Fail-Secure, Fail-Operational

A design must say what the system does when a part of it fails. There are three answers.

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Fail-Safe" icon="approve">
    On failure, the system goes to the state that does least harm. A cold room alarm is wired so that a broken wire or a loss of power raises the alarm. A pump stops when its level sensor gives no signal.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Fail-Secure" icon="approve">
    On failure, the system stays locked. A store room door remains locked when the power fails. The priority is to prevent entry.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Fail-Operational" icon="approve">
    On failure, the system continues at reduced capacity. A site with two pumps runs on one. A generator carries the essential loads when the grid fails.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

The choice between them is an ethical decision. A fire exit must fail open, so that anyone can leave, even in a building where every other door fails secure.

### Safety Margins Are Not Optional

A beam must carry 50 kN and is designed to withstand 100 kN. The extra capacity is not waste. It covers what you do not know:

- the true load
- the true strength of the material
- corrosion, fatigue and wear over the years
- loads that nobody expected
- variation in manufacture

A margin that is cut to save cost has been spent, not saved. Where a failure would endanger a person, the margin stays, whatever the budget.

### The Swiss Cheese Model

James Reason's Swiss cheese model explains how serious accidents happen in systems that have several protections. Each layer of defense (design review, testing, operating procedures, alarms) has holes. Most of the time a hole in one layer is covered by the next layer. An accident happens when the holes line up.

```text
           Layer 1    Layer 2    Layer 3
           Design     Testing    Operation
              │          │          │
  hazard ────>│          │          │   stopped
              │          │          │
  hazard ─────○─────────>│          │   stopped
              │          │          │
  hazard ─────○──────────○─────────>│   stopped
              │          │          │
  hazard ─────○──────────○──────────○──> ACCIDENT
              │          │          │
```

Each circle is a hole in a layer. Every case study in this lesson fits the model:

| Case | Layer 1 Failure | Layer 2 Failure | Layer 3 Failure |
|------|----------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Therac-25 | Software race condition | No hardware interlocks | Manufacturer dismissed incident reports |
| 737 MAX | Single-sensor MCAS design | Inadequate pilot training | FAA delegated oversight to Boeing |
| VW Emissions | Engineers wrote defeat device | Internal compliance failed to catch it | Regulators relied on lab tests only |

The model also shows why a silenced alarm matters. An alarm fitted after an accident is a new layer. When operators silence it because it sounds too often, the layer is still on the drawing and no longer in the plant. Nobody decided to remove a protection, and it has been removed.

## The Trolley Problem Is Irrelevant

<InArticleAd />


Philosophy courses often discuss the trolley problem: should you turn a runaway trolley so that it kills one person and not five? It is of little use to a working engineer. Real engineering ethics is rarely a choice of who is harmed. It is:

- following the procedures for design, test and review
- an independent check before the work reaches the field
- reporting results accurately, including the inconvenient ones
- recording decisions and the reasons for them
- saying what you do not know

Where these are in place, the dramatic choice seldom arises.

## Monitoring and data: new questions

<InArticleAd />


A monitoring system on a pump or a cold room measures machines. It also records people: when the door was opened, who was on shift when the alarm sounded, how long the operator took to answer.

### Who Is Responsible?

When a remote system shows a fault and nobody acts, who answers for it: the installer, the person who set the limits, the operator on duty, or the owner? Decide before the first alarm. Write down who receives each alarm, who must act, and within what time. An alarm that goes to everyone belongs to no one.

### Data Ethics

Door records and temperature records, taken together, show when each person arrives, how often the door is left open, and on whose shift. Data collected to protect the stock can be used to judge the staff. That may be fair, but people should be told first. Operators who feel that a system is used against them learn to defeat it: the door sensor gets a magnet, and the readings are written from memory.

### Privacy by Design

Protections for people are cheapest when they are part of the first design:

| Principle | Implementation |
|-----------|---------------|
| Data minimization | Collect only what the function needs |
| Purpose limitation | Use data only for the stated purpose |
| Storage limitation | Delete data when it is no longer needed |
| Anonymization | Remove names where the function does not need them |
| Access control | Limit who can see which data, and record who looked |
| Transparency | Tell people what is collected and why |

In many jurisdictions (EU's GDPR, Kenya's Data Protection Act, California's CCPA), these are also legal requirements.

## An Ethics Checklist for Design Review

<InArticleAd />


These questions fit any design review or handover:

<Steps>
1. **Who is affected?** The users, the people nearby, the people whose data is recorded, and the people who will maintain it.
2. **What happens if it fails?** List the failure modes. Which ones endanger a person?
3. **What are the safety margins?** Are they enough for what is unknown? Has anyone pressed you to reduce them?
4. **Is there an independent check?** Has someone outside the team reviewed the parts that matter for safety?
5. **Are the test results reported honestly?** Including the results that do not support the design, and the tests that were not done?
6. **What data does the system collect?** Who can see it? Could it be used for a purpose beyond the first one?
7. **Who cannot speak for themselves?** Children, old people, people with disabilities, future generations. Have their needs been considered?
8. **Would you be comfortable if this decision were public?** Could you defend the trade-offs to the people they affect?
</Steps>

A checklist does not replace judgment. It makes sure that the questions are asked when time is short.

### Building an Ethics Culture

One person without authority can still change how a team behaves:

- When someone raises a safety concern, thank them in front of the others.
- If nobody asks "what could go wrong?", ask it yourself.
- When cost and safety are traded against each other, write down the decision and the reason: "We chose X over Y because Z." The next person can then see what was decided and why.
- Help younger colleagues to see that "the client wants it this way" does not settle every question.

An organization where concerns are dismissed again and again will one day produce its own Therac-25. One where they are heard avoids it, and the reason is its habits, not cleverer engineers.

## Exercises

<InArticleAd />


1. Read Nancy Leveson's report on the Therac-25 incidents (available online as "Medical Devices: The Therac-25"). Identify three points where a different technical decision would have prevented harm to a patient. For each, explain the pressure that led to the actual decision.

2. Research the Challenger disaster and Roger Boisjoly's role. Write one page on the decision made on the night before the launch. Who decided? What information was available? What was the pressure?

3. Choose a monitoring system that you use or know. List everything it records about people, as distinct from machines. For each item, name one use beyond the stated purpose that could harm the people recorded.

4. Take a test sheet or a report that you signed recently. Mark each line as "I measured this", "someone told me" or "I assumed". What would you write differently?

## Summary

<InArticleAd />


An engineer owes five things: the safety of people first, honesty about what was and was not measured, work within their competence, a voice when there is danger, and records that are true. The Therac-25 shows a safety function left to software alone, and field reports that were not believed. The 737 MAX shows one unchecked sensor and concerns that were overruled. Volkswagen shows a test result made to deceive. The same duties apply to a commissioning sheet. Design for safety, with fail-safe states, margins and independent checks, means that fewer people ever face the hardest choice.
